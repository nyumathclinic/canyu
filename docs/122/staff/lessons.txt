Lesson 1: Review of the Definite Integral and the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
=================================================================================

:MW Date: January 21
:TR Date: January 20-22
:Duration: varies
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 5.1, 5.2, 5.3, 5.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand the definition of the *definite integral* of a function
  function over a closed interval.

* Understand and use the linearity and comparison properties of the
  definite integral.

* Use the Evaluation Theorem to evaluate definite integrals.

* Write antiderivatives as *indefinite integrals*.

* Interpret definite integrals as "net change" of a function over an
  interval.

* Given an integral and a substitution, transform the integral into an
  equivalent one using a substitution

* Evaluate indefinite integrals using the method of substitution.

* Evaluate definite integrals using the method of substitution.

* Use symmetry to simplify integrals of even and odd functions.


Notes
-----

All students will have seen this in a previous class, be it their high
school calculus class or our Calc I.  So don't worry about it being a
rather fast review of a very important concept.

A big important idea about the integral is that it generalizes any computation
that involves multiplication.  Area is length times width when those
are constants, but when one isn't you divide into small pieces,
approximate by assuming the pieces are constant, and then take the
limit over smaller and smaller pieces.  Distance traveled is speed
times time, when speed is constant; if not, the same
divide-and-conquer process leads to an integral.  Population is
population density times area; Mass is mass density times
length/area/volume; Total cost is marginal cost times quantity, etc.
If students only think about the integral as area it will be hard for
them to compose one that computes work (force times distance).

TR sections will have one extra class period for this material (but
note that January 20 is Inauguration Day, which students will possibly
be very interested in).  You could spend extra time on the even and
odd functions part of substitution, or go over definite integral
substitution some more.

Lesson 2: Integration by Parts
==============================

:MW Date: January 27
:TR Date: January 28
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 6.1

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand and be able to explain the *integration by parts* formula

  .. latex::

     \int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du

* Evaluate antiderivatives using integration by parts.

* Evaluate definite integrals using integration by parts.

* Evaluate integrals using integration by parts more than once.

* Evaluate integrals using a substitution followed by integration by
  parts.

Notes
-----

Every rule of antidifferentiation is a rule of differentiation in
reverse.  First, we did integration by substitution, which is the
reverse of the chain rule.  Next we do integration by parts, which is
the reverse of the product rule.

[In fact, it's been said that these are the only real "techniques of
integration."  Trigonometric substitution is just another kind of
substitution, and integration by partial fractions is just algebra.]

The standard way to derive integration by parts is to take the product
rule and integrate it:

  .. latex::

     uv = \int (uv)'\,dx = \int u'v \,dx + \int uv'\,dx

Then you solve for `$\int uv'\,dx$`.  At some point, maybe not right
away, you can show them the `$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$` form of
the equation.

The first example should be simple, for instance `$\int xe^x
\,dx$`. As you progress, try to get them thinking about how to decide
which factor should be `$u$`.  Do an example such as `$\int x^2
e^x\,dx$` where they have to integrate by parts twice, an example such
as `$\int e^x \sin x\,dx$` where they have to integrate by parts twice
then solve for the original integral, and an example where `$dv = dx$`
works.

Some students are going to know an acronym like **LIPET**.  It's
supposed to order functions by their usefulness as `$u$` when
integrating by parts.  Viz.:

* Logarithmic functions

* Inverse trigonometric functions

* Polynomial functions

* Exponential functions

* Trigonometric functions

For instance, an inverse trig function becomes an algebraic function
after differentiating, which we would hope would be simpler to
integrate once multiplied by `$v$`.  Exponential and trig functions
remain exponential and trig functions after differentiating, so they
don't get any simpler.

I don't much care for mnemonics like these; I just try to get them to
think about the choice between differentiating and antidifferentiating a
factor.  If differentiating is much easier than antidifferentiating,
than make it `$u$`.  But if they know it you can at least acknowledge
that it's useful.

Another trick some of them will know is sometimes called |tabular
integration by parts|_.  With this, you form a two-column table with your
original factors `$u$` and `$dv$` in the first row.  For each
successive row, you differentiate the first column of the previous row
and antidifferentiate the second column.  For instance:

+---------+-----------------------+
| `$u$`   | `$dv$`                |
+=========+=======================+
| `$x^2$` | `$\sin 3x$`           |
+---------+-----------------------+
| `$2x$`  | `$-\frac13 \cos 3x$`  |
+---------+-----------------------+
| `$2$`   | `$-\frac19 \sin 3x$`  |
+---------+-----------------------+
| `$0$`   | `$\frac1{27}\cos 3x$` |
+---------+-----------------------+

From this point onward the first column is going to be zero.  Then you
take the alternating sum of the products of each entry in the first
column with the entry one row below in the second column.  Thus:

.. latex::

   \int x^2 \sin 3x\,dx = x^2\left(-\frac13\right)\cos3x
   -(2x)\left(-\frac19\right)\sin3x
   +2\left(\frac1{27}\right) \cos 3x
   = -\frac13 x^2 \cos 3x + \frac29 \sin 3x + \frac{2}{27} \cos 3x

This technique was popularized from the movie |Stand and Deliver|_. It
isn't anything more than iterated integration by parts, just arranged
in columns.  And it's not as useful as it seems.  For instance, it
won't work with integrals like `$\int e^{ax} \sin bx \,dx$`, since the
first column never becomes zero. 

[Actually, it kind of works; the alternating sum turns into a
geometric series.  The series probably diverges, but if it did converge (that
is, if you do the regular thing with geometric series), it converges to
the right answer.]

.. |Stand and Deliver| replace:: :title-reference:`Stand and Deliver`
.. _Stand and Deliver: http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stand_and_Deliver

.. |tabular integration by parts| 
   replace:: *tabular integration by parts*

.. _tabular integration by parts: http://marauder.millersville.edu/~bikenaga/calc/parts/partspf.html



Lesson 3: Trigonometric Integrals and Substitution
==================================================

:MW Date: January 29
:TR Date: January 30
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 6.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Evaluate integrals of the form `$\int \sin^n x\cos^m x\,dx$`

* Evaluate integrals of the form `$\int \tan^n x \sec^m x\,dx$`

* Use the substitution `$x=a \sin \theta$` to evaluate integrals
  involving the quadratic form `$a^2-x^2$`

* Use the substitution `$x=a \tan \theta$` to evaluate integrals
  involving the quadratic form `$a^2+x^2$`

* Use the substitution `$x=a \sec \theta$` to evaluate integrals
  involving the quadratic form `$x^2-a^2$`

Notes
-----

This is hard stuff on both major topics:

The trigonometric integrals material involves a lot of trigonometric
identities, inevitably prompting the "will we need to know
trigonometric identities for the test?" question.  The answer is that
they will be required to use them, and they can either memorize them
or the key ones and derive the rest.  There is a list of trig
identities in the endpapers of the book (opposite the title page).

Trigonometric substitution is a completely different animal from the
substitution found in 5.6 (often called "`$u$`\ -substitution" in the
USA because the new variable is often named `$u$`).

Luckily, each item has their cases, so it shouldn't be too hard to
give them a box to study.  Do lots of examples and they should get
more and more comfortable.

Incidentally, this is where we can prove the area of a circle of
radius 1 is `$\pi$`.


Lesson 4: Integration by Partial Fraction Decomposition
=======================================================

:MW Date: February 2
:TR Date: February 3
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 6.3

Prerequisites
-------------

* Given a polynomial, factor it into a product of polynomials which
  are either *linear* of the form :latex:`$x-a$` or *irreducible
  quadratic* of the form :latex:`$x^2 + bx + c$`, where
  :latex:`$b^2-4c < 0$`

* Given an irreducible quadratic polynomial :latex:`$x^2 + bx+c$`,
  write it in the form :latex:`$u^2 + c$` (in other words, *complete
  the square*) 

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Given a rational function, write the form of its *partial fraction
  decomposition* into a sum of simpler rational functions, in the case
  that the denominator has

  - only linear factors, each of which is unique;
  - linear factors with repetition;
  - quadratic factors, each of which is unique;
  - quadratic factors with repetition.

* Given a rational function and the form of its partial fraction
  decomposition, find the explicit decomposition.

* Given a rational function, use its partial fraction decomposition to
  find its antiderivative.

Notes
-----

The Partial Fraction Decomposition is related to the decomposition of
an ideal in a Noetherian ring into prime ideals.  You might also want
to talk about `Egyptian fractions`_, which is a method of representing
rational numbers as sums of unit fractions: e.g., `$\frac{2}{3} =
\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6}$`.

.. _`Egyptian fractions`: http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_fraction

There will be algebraic barriers; students will not always be
confident with factoring polynomials  Teach them whatever tricks you
can.  Here's one: if the coefficients of a polynomial are integers,
and the roots are integers (obviously, you don't know this
immediately, but it's a good guess in a calculus course), the roots
divide the constant term.  So look at the factors `$d$` of the
constant term and try to divide by `$x-d$`.  Students may not know how
to do long division of polynomials, either.

Once you have the form of the PFD set up, there are some tricks to
finding the partial fraction numerators.  First you collect the terms
of the PFD together to a single fraction, and you equate the numerator
polynomials of each side.  This is an equation of polynomials, so two
things are true:

* The equation is true for all values of the variable `$x$`;

* All coefficients of the polynomials must be equal.

So one technique is to substitute for `$x$` various values that make
the equations for the partial fraction numerators just pop out.  Like
the the roots themselves.  This will in fact give you all you need if
there are no repeated roots.  If you really like this techniques, and
the roots are repeated, you can *differentiate* and apply it again.

The other technique to find the numerators is to take this equation of
polynomials and multiply it all out.  It leaves `$n$` independent equations in
`$n$` unknowns, so you can solve them.


Lesson 5: Integration with Tables; Strategies for Integration
=============================================================

:MW Date: February 4
:TR Date: February 5
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 6.4

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Find antiderivatives using a table and recognizing patterns

* Employ strategies for integration.

Notes
-----

Mathematicians are well-trained at recognizing the "form" of an
expression, and can find it in an table.  For instance, if we had to
evaluate `$\int x^3 \sin 4x\,dx$`, we would be happy if we found
`$\int u^m \sin au \,du$` in the table.  But students need a little
help in this kind of pattern-recognition.

The rest of the time, see if you can give them some guidelines on how
to attack an integral when you're not doing problems from the section
on a particular integration technique.  When should one try
integration by parts?  Trig substitution? and so on.

It's been said that there are really only two techniques of
integration: parts and substitution (partial fractions is *algebra*
followed by substitution).  Parts is particularly useful when you have
factors which get simpler upon differentiation or
anti-differentiation.  Trig substitution is the only way out of
quadratic forms.  Give them a worksheet of different integrals and
work in groups.

Lesson 6: Approximate Integration
=================================

:MW Date: February 9
:TR Date: February 10
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 6.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Use the *Trapezoidal Rule* to estimate the integral of a function.

* Use the *Midpoint Rule* to estimate the integral of a function.

* Use *Simpson's Rule* to estimate the integral of a function.

* Estimate the error in the integral approximations.

* Estimate an integral within a prescribed error.

Notes
-----

One of the more startling results about approximate integration is how
well the Midpoint Rule works, compared to the Trapezoidal Rule.  This
is basically because the endpoints aren't so far away from the
midpoint as they are from each other, and so the variation is bounded
by a smaller amount.  Morever, if a function is monotone on a
subinterval, you can see that the midpoint rule undercounts one one
side and overcounts on the other, so there's some nice cancellation.

The other startling results is how well Simpson's Rule.  By
approximating a function with a piecewise *quadratic*, we get
exactness for *cubics*.  So the error is `$O(\Delta x^4)$`, very nice
indeed.  

I like to approximate integrals that come out to nice things, such as
`$\int_0^1 \frac{4}{1+x^2}\,dx = \pi$`.  If you're technology-prone,
you can demonstrate with calculator or computer that the various
numerical algorithms do converge, and how quickly they do so.

There exist some "pure calculus" proofs of the error estimates that
are accessible to the students, but only barely.  If you're
interested, let me know, but proving the estimates is not the point.

I like to say that calculus students get nervous the moment the `$<$` sign
goes on the board.  This introduces a bit of ambivalence, nonlinear
thinking, and more than one way to the "correct" answer.  The process
of controlling the error by bounding it above with something that can
be controlled is a technique you had to learn as well.  So try to
think of the process and articulate it clearly.


Lesson 7: Improper Integrals
============================

:MW Date: February 11
:TR Date: February 12
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 6.6

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Use the definition of convergence to determine whether an improper integral converges.

* Use the `$p$`-test to determine whether an improper integral
  converges.

* Use the comparison test to decide whether an improper integral converges

Notes
-----

I often start the discussion of improper integrals with the thought
experiment of kicking the Earth into Mars's orbit, then into in
Jupiter's, etc.  In fact, you can imagine being able to kick Earth
completely out of solar orbit with a finite amount of energy (at some
point it would be far enough away to not be attracted to the sun any
more).  Each of these energies is expressed as the integral of the
gravitational force, so you have to accept that the total amount of
energy to move Earth anywhere is finite.

It will take a full class period to do both "types" of improper
integrals: Integrals over infinite intervals ("Type I") and integrals
up to an asymptote ("Type II").  

As you do the comparison test, advise students on how to pick out the
most important term in an expression.  For instance, if the integral
is `$\int_1^infty \frac{\sin^2x}{x^2+3x+4}$`, the `$x^2$` in the
denominator is what tells you to compare with
`$\int_1^\infty\frac{1}{x^2}$` (they will always forget the easiest
bound ever: that `$|\sin x| \leq 1$`.)


Lesson 8: Areas between Curves
==============================

:MW Date: February 18
:TR Date: February 17
:Duration: varies
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 7.1

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Find the area of a region by representing it as a sum of one or more
  integrals.

Notes
-----

Monday, February 16 is a holiday (Presidents' Day) throwing the schedule out of
whack this week.  But this section can be be well done in a half class or a full class.

Students latch on to the notion that the integral "is" "area under a
curve", or area between the graph and the `$x$`\ -axis.  It's often
easier to integrate the difference between functions and let go of the
axis altogether.  After all, translating the region may move it in
relation to the axis, but doesn't change its area.  

Get them to think about integrating in the vertical direction as well
as horizontally.  Find an example where computing the area with
horizontal integrations takes two integrals but only one vertically.

Lesson 9: Volumes
=================

:MW Date: February 18
:TR Date: Feburary 19
:Duration: varies
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 7.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Find the volume of a solid by slicing.

* Find the volume of a solid of revolution using the disc or washer
  method.

Notes
-----

Again, you can do this in a half or a full class period.

It seems that AP Calculus emphasizes more the solids of revolution
than general solids, for which the volume can be computed by slicing.
So students who had been comfortable up to now might suddenly feel
lost.  

But finding volumes by slicing is an important skill because students
will have to "invent" an integral each time rather than simply
remember a formula and plug it in.  Deriving the integrand is also
going to take some work because it often depends on some geometry
techqniques (similar triangles, the pythagorean theorem, etc.) that
they learned a long time ago.

Do a pyramid first, I'd say.  You illustrate the technique, and
reinforce faith in it by deriving a recognizable formula.  Here's a
good place to praise calculus: as far as I know, in contrast to plane
polygons, there is no finite-dissection argument which will derive the
formula for the volume of a tetrahedron or pyramid.  You have to
integrate something.  


Lesson 10: Volumes by Cylindrical Shells
========================================

:MW Date: February 23
:TR Date: February 24
:Duration: one half of one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 7.3

Learning Objective
------------------

* Find the volume of a solid of revolution using the shell method.

Notes
-----

When finding the volume of a solid of revolution, students who want to
latch onto a formula will find it hard to do so.  There are many
parameters to take into account—the functions involved, the axis
direction, the axis position, the method chosen (washer or shell).
Try to discourage formula memorization and encourage the process,
which is actually quite easy when you get the hang of it.

Students who have had a light version of calculus will be comfortable
with revolutions about either axis but not around a different
horizontal or vertical line.  Do a few examples of these to make sure
they see when and why `$x$` needs to be changed to `$x-a$` (or whatever).

3D models would be great here.
  
Lesson 10: Arc length
=====================

:MW Date: February 23
:TR Date: February 24
:Duration: one-half of one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 7.4

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* find the length of the graph of a function.

Notes
-----

The derivation is the more important thing here, because it's another
example of an integral which does not represent plain/plane area.
Moreover, once you have the formula, there are not too many functions
whose graphs you can find the length of by elemetary integration.  You
could, however, refer back to numerical techniques if you wanted.

Do not use this formula to "prove" that the circumference of a unit
circle is `$2\pi$`; this is circular (no pun intended) because that's
the definition of `$\pi$`.


Lesson 11: Applications to Physics
==================================

:MW Date: February 25–March 2
:TR Date: February 26–March 3
:Duration: 1.5 class periods
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 7.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Use Riemann sums to derive integrals from problems.

* Compute the work needed to move a particle against a varying force field.

* Compute the work needed to move a body of solid/fluid in irregular
  shape and or density a certain distance.

* Compute the center of mass of a planar lamina of uniform density.


Notes
-----

Here are two more applications of integration in which an integral is
derived that does not measure area (or volume, for that matter).  I
have found that students need more than one class period (i.e., some
time to think) to "get" the idea of work and computing it by an
integral.  

Do not assume that your students are all aware or comfortable with
physics.  Explain the concept of work for constant forces, like
gravity.  Lifting up an object to a table takes work, dropping it down
to the ground is work done by the Earth, and sliding it along the top
of a frictionless table takes no work.  

The center of mass of a planar lamina (or the centroid of a region,
which is the same thing assuming the region has uniform density), is
another nice application.  The COM is the place where the region will
"balance" if you stick it on the point of a pencil; a demonstration
would be effective here.

Hydrostatic force is also in this section; let's skip it.

You should also have some time on March 2 or 3 to review for the midterm.


Lesson 12: Differential Equations
=================================

:MW Date: October 20
:TR Date: October 21
:Duration: two class periods
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 7.6

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand and recognize a *differential equation* and *initial
  value problem*.

* Construct an ODE from a word problem.

* Solve separable differential equations exactly.

* Sketch the *slope field* of an ordinary differential equation.

* Use the slope field of an ODE to sketch solution curves to initial
  value problems.

Notes
-----

There is a lot one could do with differential equations!  A major goal
should be to understand how various "natural" laws manifest themselves
as differential equations.  For instance, the mixing problems
described in this section are all examples of the rule that "(net
change of stuff) is (rate of stuff in) minus (rate of stuff out)".
Another very common example of a scientific law begetting a
differential equation is Newton's Second Law `$F=ma$`.  In fact,
Newton actually wrote it in :title-reference:`Principia` as a
first-order ODE by saying Force was the change in *momentum.*  

Another important point, I think, is that "most" differential
equations are not solvable exactly.  We have graphical techniques to
analyze solutions, and numerical techniques to find the solutions to
arbitrary accuracy, but in the universe of ODEs only a set of measure
zero are integrable in elementary terms.  This is why I tend to
emphasize the non-analytic techniques on the first day, then get back
to the separation-of-variables trick later.  

You can talk about equilibrium solutions to homogeneous ODEs if you
like, but there are no problems on that in the book so we won't test them.

Lesson 13: Sequences
====================

:MW Date: March 23
:TR Date: March 24
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.1

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand the definition and recognize a *sequence*.

* Use algebraic simplification to find the limit of a sequence.

* Use calculus to find the limit of a sequence.

Notes
-----

You will discover that students are conditioned to be afraid of this
chapter.  All their friends (who are not math majors, and maybe even
some who are) who have passed through calculus have told them this is
hard stuff.  And, I think that the way the curriculum is designed,
with this front-loading of the concepts, students are drowning in new
ideas by the time they get to Taylor Series.

Try to reframe the chapter in the same way as some of the other
chapters.  The derivative is an invention to solve the *tangent
problem*.  The integral in an invention to solve the *area problem*.
The big tool in this chapter, as far as the study of functions goes,
is the *power series*, and the problem we are trying to solve is
approximating complicated functions by simple ones (polynomials).  It
will take us a while to get there, but if you choose examples that
foreshadow this major concept ahead, you can keep them focused on the
big picture.

For this section, the big idea is the definition of sequence, finding
limits of a sequence, and the theorem that a bounded monotone sequence
converges.  You can emote (but don't give the details) that this is a
fundamental property of *real* numbers. There are lots of bounded
increasing sequences of rational numbers that don't converge to
rational numbers.  


Lesson 14: Series
=================

:MW Date: March 25
:TR Date: March 26
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

*  Understand that an infinite sum can converge to a finite value.

*  Know the definition of a convergent series.

*  Know or be able to derive the formulae

   .. latex::
    \sum_{k=0}^{n-1}ar^k=a\frac{1-r^n}{1-r}
   
   and

   .. latex::

    \sum_{k=0}^\infty ar^k=\frac{a}{1-r}

   when `$|r|<1$`.

* Be able to use the above formulae in calculations.


Notes
-----

We tend to make a big deal that it's counterintuitive that an infinite
set of positive numbers can add up to something finite.  I think
students actually want to believe this.  So make sure you present both
sides of the argument.

But a nice proof that infinite sums are possible is cutting a cake.
Give half to the first person in the class, a fourth to the second
person, an eighth to the third, and so on.  You can give everyone a
slice no matter how many people you have in the class, and you will
always have some left over.  However, the amount left over approaches
zero (that is, the amount distributed approaches the full cake), so
for an infinite class you will serve the entire cake. 


Lesson 15: The Integral and Comparison Tests
============================================

:MW Date: March 30
:TR Date: March 31
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.3

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Use the *integral test* to decide if a series converges.

* Use the *comparison test* to decide if a series converges.

* Use the *limit comparison test* to decide if a series converges.


Notes
-----

Students will like the integral test because it's conclusive.
Integrate it and if the improper integral converges, the so does the
series, and if the integral diverges, so does the series.

But emphasize that the integral test doesn't give you any idea what
the series converges *to*, so you've done a lot of work to integrate
something then thrown away the product.  The comparison test allows to
you to do *easy* integrals, like `$p$`-integrals, instead if all you want
to do is decide convergence or divergence.

Remind them how in the section on improper integrals we "eyeballed"
the integrand to pull out the dominant term and decide convergence; we
then used that term to construct something against which to compare.
So it is with series.  When deciding the convergence of `$\sum
\frac{1}{n^2+2}$`, sure, you could integrate `$\int_0^\infty
\frac{dx}{x^2+2}$`, but the comparison test gets the answer faster.

It's worth pointing out that we've subtly changed the acceptable
answers to the problem we're doing here.  For derivatives, integrals,
etc., we've usually wanted a number for an answer.  That's often too
hard for series, so we just ask a simpler equation: finite or
infinite?  When your standards are so low, there's more than way to
get there.

The limit comparison test is there because sometimes the obvious
comparisons don't go in the right direction.  For instance, consider
`$\sum \frac{1}{n+2}$`.  You'd like to compare it to
`$\sum\frac{1}{n}$` which we know diverges, but
`$\frac{1}{n+2}<\frac{1}{n}$` for `$n >0$`.  In effect, we'd be saying
that `$\sum \frac{1}{n+2}$`, if it exists, is less than or equal to
`$\infty$`.  A standard trick out of this is to scale: while
`$\frac{1}{n+2}<\frac{1}{n}$`, we have that
`$\frac{1}{2n}<\frac{1}{n+2}$` eventually, and this inquality goes in
the right direction to apply the Comparison Test.  The LCT is just the
formalization of this trick.

Make sure you keep your language clean, too.  Students have trouble
distinguishing between sequences and series.  It doesn't help when we
speak of the general term of a series as representing the series
itself.  We might say "`$\frac{1}{n}$` diverges" when we mean "the
*series* `$\sum\frac{1}{n}$` diverges".  An expert would be able to
discern that from the context, but not calculus learners, who might
respond with "I thought you said last time that `$\frac{1}{n}$`
converges to zero?"  (She's thinking of the sequence whose `$n$`\ th
term is `$\frac{1}{n}$`, which does converge to zero.)  If you train
yourself to say "the *sequence* of `$\frac{1}{n}$` converges", but
"the *series* on `$\sum\frac{1}{n}$` diverges," you will help them
keep track.  They might even learn to ask, "Do you mean the sequence
or the series?" when presented with a "bald" expression like `$\frac{1}{n}$`.


Lesson 16: Other convergence tests
==================================

:MW Date: April 1
:TR Date: April 2
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.4

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand the definition of and recognize an *alternating series*

* Use the *Alternating Series Test* to decide the convergence of an
  alternating series.

* Estimate the remainder of a partial sum of an alternating series.

* Estimate the sum of an alternating series to a prescribed accuracy
  by bounding the remainder.

* Understand and apply the definition of *absolute convergence* for a series
  having negative and positive terms.

* Use the *Ratio Test* to decide if a series converges (absolutely)

Notes
-----

I think we can skip the Root Test.

Tell students that the dichotomy (convergent or divergent) for series
containing only positive terms is really a trichotomy for series with
terms of both signs.  A series can converge absolutely, converge
conditionally, or diverge.  Make sure they know of at least one
conditionally convergent series (e.g., the alternating harmonic
series).

They will also get tripped up on the logic behind the Ratio Test.
They might get the idea that if the absolute value of the ratio of
consecutive terms has a limit of one, then one cannot decide if the
series converges.  We very likely *can* decide; we just can't use the
Ratio Test to decide.  Show them that for any `$p$`-series, the ratio
always approaches one, but the convergence is well-understood by the
`$p$`-test.  This demonstrates why the Ratio Test is inconclusive, as
well as what inconclusive means.


Lesson 17: Power Series
=======================

:MW Date: April 6
:TR Date: April 7
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand the definition of and recognize a *power series*.

* Find the *radius of convergence* and *integral of convergence* of a
  power series.

Notes
-----

Power series are series on steroids.  You can test the convergence or
divergence at any value of the variable.  And it's really
remarkable—or at least it should be—that the set of numbers for
which the series converges is an interval.  

Demonstrate this with some indirect reasoning about power series.  If
the power series is centered at 1 and converges at 3 but diverges at
-3, we can conclude it converges at 0 and diverges at 6, for instance.

Some numerical demonstration helps here, too.  Consider the power
series `$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n}$`.  Plug in `$x=1$`
and you get the alternating harmonic series, which converges very
slowly to `$\ln 2$`.  Plug in `$x=-\frac{1}{2}$` and you get
convergence to `$-\ln 2$`, but much more quickly.  Plug in `$x=-1$` and
you get the harmonic series, for which the terms do tend to zero but
which doesn't converge.  Plug in `$x=2$` and you get a series where
the terms start to blow up.  So it's all there.


Lesson 18: Representing Functions as Power Series
=================================================

:MW Date: April 8
:TR Date: April 9
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Given a function and a point inside its domain, find a power series
  which converges to the given function on an open interval centered at
  the given point.  This is called briefly a *power series
  representation* of the function at the point.

* Integrate and differentiate power series.

Notes
-----

The really big idea here is that if a function has a power series
representation, its derivatives and antiderivatives do, too, and they
are arrived at the in the obvious way, by differentiating or
antidifferentiating term-by-term.  This is not true for every series
of functions, so make sure your students appreciate the depth of this
theorem (not that you have to prove it or anything).

This is where you can start to have fun with convergent series.  It's
now possible to show, for instance, that the alternating harmonic
series converges to `$\ln 2$`.  Can they find one that converges to
`$\ln 3$`? Or you can show them `Euler-Machin formulas`_ and derive
series of rational numbers which converge to `$\pi$`.  They all come
out of this process of taking a "geometric" power series, and
integrating or differentating it to arrive at power series
representation for something else.

.. _`Euler-Machin formulas`: http://mathworld.wolfram.com/EulersMachin-LikeFormula.html

If you haven't told them yet, tell them that `$\sum_{n=1}^\infty
\frac{1}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}$`.  So this technique, while powerful,
won't sum every series.  That should blow their mind.  You can go on
about the zeta function if you want, too.


Lesson 19: Taylor and Maclaurin Series
======================================

:MW Date: April 13
:TR Date: April 14
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.7

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Compute the Taylor series of a function at a point.  

* Compute derivatives at a point using a Taylor series.

Notes
-----

There is an pseudo-algorithm for computing the Taylor series of a function.
You take repeated derivatives, evaluate them at the point you want to
center the series, and use those derivatives to build a series.  If
you are lucky, you recognize a pattern and you can write down the
general term in the series in some closed form.  

But that really only works for since, cosine, and the exponential
function.  In all the rest, the relevant theorem is that *if a
function has a power series representation near a point, it is*
**equal** *to the Taylor series at that point.*  So one can look at
the power series and read off the derivatives.  

I am always frustrated by students who one day can find a series which
converges to the actan function but the next day, when asked to find
the Taylor series for arctan, will start to make a table of
derivatives and give up around the point they have to start using the
Quotient Rule.  That's the whole point; there are better ways to do it.


Lesson 20: Applications of Taylor Polynomials
=============================================

:MW Date: April 15
:TR Date: April 16
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 8.7

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Estimate functions using a Taylor series.

* Solve differential equations using power series.

Notes
-----

Don't do the relativity stuff here.  Focus on using Taylor polynomials to
estimate values of functions and talk about expressing solutions to an
ODE as power series.  

This brings up an answer to the question "What is this good for?"  You
can ask them about the sine function, and how they think their
calculator comes up with an answer when you ask it to find the sine of
some angle.  We know that the sine of an angle `$x$` a "the ratio of
the opposite side to the hypotenuse, when one angle of a right
triangle is `$x$`."  But calculators don't draw little right triangles
and measure them.  Or we can think about the sine of `$x$` as "the
vertical coordinate of a point on the unit circle, for which the arc
from (1,0) to the given point is `$x$` long."  But does the calculator
draw a little unit circle?

No, a calculator computes the value of sine by evaluating some
convergent series.  It's a limit of polynomials, in other words,
approximated by sums and products, which calculators definitely can do!


Lesson 21: Parametric Curves
============================

:MW Date: April 20
:TR Date: April 21
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 9.1

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Given parametric equations for `$x$` and `$y$`, find and sketch the
  parametric curve they represent.

* Given a curve in the plane, find a pair of parametric equations for
  it.

Notes
-----

Students will find this harder than you think.  Maybe you found it
hard the first time around.

I spend a lot of time drawing a nodal cubic based on its
parametrization: `$x=t^2-1$`, `$y=t^2-t$`.  This shows how different
parametric curves can be from graphs of functions.  Or you could do
Lissajous figures.  Help them eliminate the parameter as a means for
recognizing the curve by its equations.

For going the other way (from the curve to the equations), they should
know how to parametrize circles and ellipses, lines, and other graphs
of functions.  That's about it, I'd say.

An `Etch A Sketch`_ might make a good demo here.  

.. _`Etch A Sketch`: http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Etch_A_Sketch


Lesson 22: Calculus with Parametric Curves
==========================================

:MW Date: April 22
:TR Date: April 23
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 9.3

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Find tangent lines to parametric curves.

* Find areas enclosed by parametric curves.

* Find lengths of parametric curves.


Notes
-----

Tangents to parametric curves come from the Chain Rule.  They may have
seen that in a previous class.  Make sure they know that you can use
it to find *vertical* tangents as well as horizontal ones.  

For areas enclosed by parametric curves, see the book for the cases in
which this is possible.  The general case is saved for Calc III—you
can tell them that.  

For lengths of parametric curves, this might actually be more
intiutive than the old (`$y=f(x)$`) way.  So it's worth doing again,
and emphasizing how it's more symmetric than the first way.

Lesson 23: Polar Coordinates
============================

:MW Date: April 27
:TR Date: April 28
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 9.3

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Convert the coordinates of a point in the plane from cartesian to
  polar and *vice versa*

* Draw plane curves described in polar coordinates.

* Find tangent lines to plane curves described in polar coordinates.

Notes
-----

Students don't seem to be able to concieve a pre-cartesian plane.  Try
to point out that there is more than one way to do things, and even
though we are more familiar with cartesian coordinates, they are no
more natural than others.  In fact, all coordinates are human
invention.  Point to the blackboard, and say, "If this is a plane,
where is the `$x$`-axis?"  Of course, there isn't one until you say
so.

Anyway, polar coordinates used to be taught more in high school than
they are now.  So students won't be as familiar with them as you might
think (or as the book might assume). 

Notice that we are going to allow the value of the axial (`$r$`) coordinate
to be negative.  This is more to give a meaning to it when it comes
out of polar equations than to find the coordinates of a specific point.
