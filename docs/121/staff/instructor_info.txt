Instructor's Guide to Calculus
!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!!

:Author: Matthew Leingang

Welcome to Courant's calculus program.  This document is a guide to
the nuts and bolts of the course and its organization.  For the
week-by-week list of topics, see the calendar.  For information and
tips on teaching, see the lesson guide.  See also the list of homework
problems to be assigned.

Topics and Goals
================

In Calc I we study functions, limits, derivatives, applications of
derivatives, the definite integral, the Fundamental Theorem of
Calculus, and some techniques of integration.  If you are familiar
with the AP Calculus courses, this is about the same list of topics as
the AB syllabus, although we will cover them to a far deeper extent
than in a high school course.  

In Calc II we study techniques of integration in earnest, applications
of integration (area, volume, mass, and some others), first-order
differential equations, sequences, series, power series, parametric
curves, and polar coordinates.  Our Calc I and II covers about the
same material as the AP Calculus BC syllabus, but again in much more
depth.  

In Calc III we study the geometry of three-dimensional space and
multivariable (mostly two- and three-variable) calculus.  Topics
include vectors in two and three dimensions, quadric surfaces,
functions of several variables, partial derivatives, gradients,
constrained and unconstrained optimization, multiple integration,
vector fields, line and flux integrals, and Green's/Gauss's/Stokes's
theorems.

A major goal of college mathematics (says the MAA) is to move
students from thinking of mathematics as a set of *procedures* to a
set of *concepts*.  So in a college course we discuss the concepts and
encourage a lot of communication.

Course Details 
==============


Class Meetings and Teams
------------------------

The generic calculus course meets in one of two varieties.

* One variety meets twice a week for 110 minutes each class period.
  The instructor presents all class material, although it may (and
  probably should) have an interactive component to it.

* The other variety meets twice a week for 75 minutes each class,
  along with a weekly 50 minute recitation section.  The instructor
  presents the main material while the TA organizes the recitations.

When at all possible, sections which meet at the same time should work
together as a team.  This is a good way for experienced faculty to
pass down wisdom to eager young faculty, and to distribute repeated
work (e.g., writing and grading of quizzes and exams).

If you are giving one of the big lectures or a recitation, you should
also be working together.  Recitation instructors should be aware of
what topics are being covered, and should prepare recitations finding
examples fitting those topics.  Lecturers should feel free to assign
certain examples or topics (provided it's not new material not covered
in lecture) to recitations.  Keep in mind that recitations can meet on
any day of the week, so they can't always be doing the exact same
thing.

Homework 
--------

Homework takes two forms: WebAssign problems which are for practice
with computation and technique, and written problems which are more
conceptual.  Usually WebAssign problems will be due the first day of
the week (Monday or Tuesday) after the material is covered, and
written problems the last day of the week (Wednesday or Thursday).

Graders are told to expect students to express ideas clearly, legibly,
and completely, often requiring complete English sentences rather than
merely just a long string of equations.  This means points could be
taken off for unexplained answers.  They may be reluctant to do this,
so give them your support.  See the homework page for a more detailed
rubric.  If students complain, point to the rubric.

In fairness to the rest of the students and to graders, late homework
will generally not be accepted.  We do drop the lowest grade, which
you can point out to students who for whatever reason need a pass.

Since all sections will be having the same problem sets, we'll have a
common Blackboard site for all sections (in addition to any Blackboard
site you want to create for your own section).  We'll hire one person
to generate solution sets for the assignments and put them on the
common Blackboard.

Encourage students to collaborate on homework and studying in general.
Many students who would benefit from group studying have not been
conditioned to do so.  Just require that what they turn in should be
written up by them in their own words, and that they list their
collaborators on the paper.

There is free math tutoring sponsored by the math department, meeting
in room 524 of Warren Weaver Hall.  Check the signs posted throughout WWH
and the `tutoring web page`__.

__ http://math.nyu.edu/degree/undergrad/tutor_schedule.html


Exams 
------

Midterm Exam
~~~~~~~~~~~~
 
There will be one midterm exam, held in class on October 14 (for MW
sections) and 15 (for TR sections).  Each section will write their own
midterm, although as suggested before sections meeting at the same
time can and should work together to save efforts.  The timing is
important: this is the latest you can give a test that will give them
some time to contemplate dropping the course if they're not keeping up
and yourself some time to prepare midterm grade reports.

We will put old midterms on the common Blackboard sites to help
students prepare.  Solutions will appear one week before the exam.

Exams should contain a mixture of computational and conceptual
problems.  It may contain some fixed-response questions (e.g.,
multiple-choice and matching) but must also have free-response
questions.  Some of them should resemble homework problems.  I am also
a fan of at least one problem that requires synthesizing multiple
concepts.  Students will resist this but gently remind them that we
want them to have some problem-solving skills, and that means solving
problems that haven't been solved before.  This is what happens in the
real world every day.

I shoot for a median score of 80% on my exams, although I confess I do
not get there often.  But this allows students to show what they know
and for the good students to distinguish themselves.  If the mean or
median score is low students get demoralized, and if the variance is
low it's hard to decide grades.

Final Exam
~~~~~~~~~~

**The final exam for all Calc I and II sections (including the evening
ones, despite what it says on the registrar's schedule) will be
Friday, December 18, from 2:00-3:50pm. Say this out loud in class on
the first day.** Some students will make plans (e.g., plane tickets)
that conflict with the final, and we cannot reschedule the final for
them.

The final exam for Calc I and II is common.  Finals for Calc III are
section specific.  Finals should be cumulative.


Quizzes
-------

You should have several quizzes to make sure students keep up and get
some feedback.  The number and schedule is up to you.  You could give
them every week or after every chapter.  The only thing is that the
syllabus promises no pop quizzes.  So you should decide when the
quizzes will be and publicize this information in advance.  I've found
every other week to be a pretty good schedule.  

For the most part quizzes should only assess material that they've
already had *returned* homework on.  So what's done in week *n* can't be
quizzed until week *n+2*.

Quiz scores will be averaged together, again dropping the lowest.


Policy on out-of-sequence exams and missed quizzes
--------------------------------------------------

This is to protect instructors from students who pull heartstrings or
are trying to work the system.

We are only able to accommodate a limited number of out-of-sequence
exams due to limited availability of rooms and proctors.  For this
reason, you may approve out-of-sequence midterm exams in the following
cases:

1.  A documented medical excuse.

2.  A University sponsored event such as an athletic tournament, a
    play, or a musical performance.  *Athletic practices and
    rehearsals do not fall into this category.* Please have your
    coach, conductor, or other faculty advisor contact your
    instructor.

3.  A religious holiday.

4.  Extreme hardship such as a family emergency. 

We must give priority to these students.  If there is space  
available, we will consider other requests.  Everyone feels that  
their request is the most important and pressing, so we consider  
these requests on a first-come, first-served basis.

Scheduled out-of-sequence exams and quizzes (those not arising from
emergencies) must be taken *before* the actual exam.  You can swear
one student to secrecy, but swearing the rest of the class is another thing.

Some students will require extra time for exams because of a learning
disability, or have other special needs.  If they tell you about this,
tell them to get in touch with the Moses Center for Student
Disabilities.  They will help you and the student make arrangements
for the student's particular needs.

Makeup midterm exams
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Use your discretion on allowing these.  If a student had some schedule conflict that they didn't tell you about (something that might fall under #2 above), I think they're not entitled to a makeup exam.  If it's a documentable medical excuse. make sure they do document it.  

Statistically speaking, a lot of students' grandparents will die during the term.  For many students, it's the first time they'll experience a death in the family.  So be sensitive to that.  Request some kind of documentation (like an obituary).

When asking for documentation, you might feel like you're telling the student that you think they're dishonest.  It helps to say that this policy is to prevent *other* students from manipulating the system.  


Makeup final exams
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Makeups for final exams should be coordinated to save work.  Since all students have the same material, one makeup exam can probably be used for all of them.  

Because final exams are so important, we should be even more formal in scheduling them.  I think it's best to give a grade of Incomplete to anyone who misses the final and then we'll schedule another one in February.

Grades
------

The final course score will be a weighted average of the various
course components (exams, homework, quizzes, etc.), with weights given
as on the syllabus.  This term the homework counts more because
they'll be doing lots of WebAssign work.  

We should coordinate our efforts in the assignment of letter grades to
scores.  Much of the grades will be based on the same material (i.e.,
the common final in Calc I).  The others can be normalized so as to be
comparable.  It's important that students who score equally in two
different sections should get comparable grades.  I

We will convert this score to a letter grade *beginning* with these
values as cutoffs:

+---------+--------------+
| Cutoff  | Letter Grade |
+=========+==============+
| 93      | A            |
+---------+--------------+
| 90      | A-           |
+---------+--------------+
| 87      | B+           |
+---------+--------------+
| 83      | B            |
+---------+--------------+
| 80      | B-           |
+---------+--------------+
| 75      | C+           |
+---------+--------------+
| 70      | C            |
+---------+--------------+
| 65      | C-           |
+---------+--------------+
| 50      | D            |
+---------+--------------+

These cutoffs might be adjusted, but only in the downward direction
(to make letter grades higher).  

The above table is public record, and I like publicizing it to give
students something to shoot for.  What's next is **top secret**: I
end up always moving down at least some of the cutoffs.  Then I keep
them secret.  That has the appearance of generosity (B+'s magically go
to A-'s), and the advantage of resumed secrecy (nobody knows the exact
cutoff so they can't grub for it).  

In practice, the exams are difficult enough I rarely feel that I've
given a student a grade higher than he or she deserved because of a
published table.  So don't take this table as overly restrictive—you
will still probably be able to give the grades you want.

Curves
~~~~~~

Students will ask if they'll be graded on a curve.  Basically, the
policy of lowering cutoffs is what they're interested in.  They will
also probably ask for the midterm exams or other assessments to be
"curved."  We know that the the variance of a sum of independent
variables is the sum of the variances, so I tell them that a single
curve at the end of the course suffices. 

When students ask about grades, yes, they want to know details, but
they also want to know how you feel they're progressing.  So if they
have a score that they feel is low, but is actually above average,
make sure they know that.  You can turn an annoying conversation about
curves into a meaningful conversation about their progress and what
they can do to accelerate it.



Techniques
==========

So you know what you have to do and shouldn't do.  But how do you do it?


Preparing for class
-------------------

You will need some time to prepare for your classes.  How much depends
on your experience.  

For Calc I and II I've put specific *learning objectives* for the
sections of the book into a big packet.  These are the things you
should make sure get covered.  (As a side note for when you plan a
course on your own: these learning objectives become a study guide
when exam time comes around).  How you get to them is up to you.

Think about the key ideas of the day, and think of key examples that
will illuminate them.  Make sure your examples are not (all) too
simple or too difficult.  If you can think a little bit ahead, try to
find examples to which you can return later on.  

In this sense, teaching a math class is no different from giving a
talk.  You give the main ideas and the key examples, and you have to
go slow because nobody knows as much about your topic as you.

When I had them, I also included in the lesson guide my thoughts on
the topics, the pitfalls I've seen students have, and how I sometimes
organize my class.  Use it as you see fit.


Class choreography
------------------

We like lectures that go definition-theorem-proof-example but we
didn't when we were learning mathematics for the first time.  Teaching
mathematics to mathematics learners requires an Aristotelian rather
than Platonic view of how we understand things.  Students would prefer
examples which guide the concept or point to the theorem, and a proof
does not convince them if they don't understand it.  So think instead
about example-example-definition-example-theorem-example(s) as an outline.

*Studies show that the style of class has a significant effect on
student learning*.  Some variables which do not have an effect are the
textbook, the stature (academically) of the teacher, the home country
of the teacher, or the time of day the course meets.  Students learn
better in a course which provides *interactive engagement*.  This
means hands-on work with the concepts and immediate feedback.  

If you are in the lecture/recitation variety, the recitation is the
prime place for interactivity.  The difficulty will be getting
students to come to recitation.  Having quizzes there helps, but it
also has to be demonstrated that recitations will help them learn.

If your class periods are 110 minutes long, that should be plenty of
time to involve some interactive components in your classes.  Please
don't lecture at them for almost two hours in a row!

Examples of interactive engagement are:

* worksheets done in class.  Discuss the solutions afterwards.

* problems worked on in groups.  Obviously, the problems could come
  from a worksheet.  When students are working in class, circulate to
  keep things moving and answer questions.

* getting students to discuss concepts.  One way to do this is to ask
  a multiple choice conceptual question, and have them vote.  Then
  have them discuss their answers, and then vote again.  Then have a
  "convert" explain why he or she changed his or her mind.

* having students work out problems on the board.  Turn the tables and
  play the role of a questioning student.

Do this early if you want to do it at all because first impressions
will count.

Blackboard
----------

Blackboard_ is a web-based course management system.  You should all
have course websites, and there's no reason to reinvent the wheel.
Blackboard is not perfect but it has lots of useful tools.  It also
handles a few major issues of putting course stuff up on the web:
access to copyrighted material and protection of students' personal
information.

.. _Blackboard: http://www.blackboard.com/

Once you get your NYUCard and NetID, you can get onto the University
intranet, which is http://home.nyu.edu/ .  From there, click the
"Academics" tab and follow the links to set up your Blackboard "shell"
(a website for your section).

There will be common blackboard sites for each Calculus course to hold
common documents such as sample exams and homework solutions.
Sections should have their own section-specific blackboards as well.

Sections which are lecture/recitation should probably merge their
Blackboard sites so that all students have a common site and a
recitation subsection.  

Blackboard sites should have *at a minimum* contact information and
office hours for the teaching staff.  There are lots of other
doohickeys you can put on them, such as:

* online forums where students can discuss topics.  Teachers should
  keep track of these to answer questions and look out for students
  sharing answers to homework. 

* chat widgets so you can have online office hours.  This might be
  useful if you want to be at home but available.

* links to other webpages.  There are millions of java and flash
  applets on the web to illustrate calculus.  If you build a library
  of links you can get students thinking about using the web
  themselves for studying.

* a blog where you can write about things tangential to the course,
  summarize a class, answer a question that got asked, or correct a
  mistake you made.  Blogging about things which aren't entirely math
  will help convince students you're human.

Be aware of copyright, a very important issue at NYU.  If you put
copyrighted material on your course website, make sure that access to
it is limited to the NYU community.  This applies to the solutions to
problem sets that we will be putting up as well.

If you are older than 30, keep in mind that students have a much
different view of the web than you do.  The web has been a major part
of communication since before these students learned how to read.  To
them it is not a new-fangled computer gizmo.  They will expect you to
have a functioning, useful website.

Facebook
--------

If you have a Facebook account, you probably will get added by some of
your students.  You should probably decide ahead of time to accept all
requests or deny all requests, to avoid the impression of playing
favorites.  

I recommend you consider the former.  You can create a custom list of
student "friends" and use it to exclude or include them from seeing
certain things you post.  Letting your students get to know you a
little better—and you getting to know them better in return—is a nice
way to make class more comfortable.  

Facebook is still relatively new and the etiquette on student/teacher
interaction is not quite worked out yet.  I think students think of
their facebook pages as their online homes or dorm rooms.  Now that
you have a job interacting with students, your page functions
something like an online office.

Conclusions
===========

The department wants coordinated calculus not to rein you in but to
give you freedom.  The drudgery, administrivia, and unexpected
consequences are lessened quite a bit by having a lot of the
organizational bits done in advance.  This also creates a fair
environment for the students, who won't get radically different math
courses only because they have different schedules.  You can focus on
the students and teaching them well.

And in that I wish you luck!  Please talk to me if you have any
questions.
