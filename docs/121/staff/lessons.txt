Lesson 1: Functions and their Representations
=============================================

:MW Date: January 21
:TR Date: January 20
:Duration: varies
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 1.1

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand the definition of *function*.

* Work with functions represented in different ways:

  - symbolically, or algebraically, i.e., by an explicit formula;

  - numerically, i.e., by a table of values;

  - graphically, i.e., by a picture of its graph;

  - verbally, i.e., a description of the function in natural language.

* Work with functions defined *piecewise* over several intervals.

* Understand and apply the definition of *increasing function*.

* Understand and apply the definition of *decreasing function*.

* Understand and apply the definition of *even function*.

* Understand and apply the definition of *odd function*.

Lesson 2: A Catalog of Essential Functions
==========================================

:MW Date: January 21
:TR Date: January 22
:Duration: varies
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 1.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Identify different classes of algebraic functions, including:

  - *polynomial* (especially *linear*, *quadratic*, and *cubic*);

  - *rational*;

  - *power*;

  - *trigonometric*;

  - *exponential*.

* Understand the effect of algebraic transformations on the graph of a
  function, for instance:

  - the graph of :latex:`$y=f(x) + c$` is the graph of `$y=f(x)$` moved
    up by `$c$`;

  - the graph of :latex:`$y=f(x) - c$` is the graph of `$y=f(x)$` moved
    down by `$c$`;

  - the graph of :latex:`$y=f(x+c)$` is the graph of `$y=f(x)$` moved
    to the left by `$c$`;

  - the graph of :latex:`$y=f(x-c)$` is the graph of `$y=f(x)$` moved
    to the right by `$c$`;

  - the graph of :latex:`$y=c f(x)$` is the graph of `$y=f(x)$` scaled
    vertically by a factor of `$c$`;

  - the graph of :latex:`$y= f(cx)$` is the graph of `$y=f(x)$` scaled
    horizontally by a factor of `$1/c$`.

* Understand and compute the *composition* of two functions.

Notes
-----

See the note above for MW sections about timing.  TR sections shoud
have plenty of time to complete a worksheet about the geometric
effects.  Students who have been conditioned to reach first for their
calculator when graphing will find it hard to think flexibly about how
graphs change when the function is changed.  Still, this is the kind
of familiarity needed to think conceptually about functions.

Lesson 3: The Limit of a Function
=================================


:MW Date: January 26
:TR Date: January 27
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 1.3

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand and state the informal definition of a limit.

* Observe limits on a graph.

* Guess limits by algebraic manipulation.

* Guess limits by numerical information.

Notes
-----

The informal definition (if that's not a contadiction in terms) in
Stewart is something like ":latex:`$\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$` if values
of `$f(x)$` can be made arbitrarily close to `$L$` by taking `$x$` to be
sufficiently close to `$a$` (and not equal to `$a$`)."  The adverbs
"arbitrarily" and "sufficiently" translate into the ":latex:`$\forall\
\epsilon > 0$`" and ":latex:`$\exists\ \delta > 0$`" statements, but
don't tell them that.  We are not going to do the precise definition
of a limit.

I like to frame this as a game between the students and me.  One team
sets the maximum error allowed—how close do you need `$f(x)$` to be to
`$L$`?  The other shows a "tolerance" interval around `$a$` such that if
`$x$` is within that interval then `$f(x)$` is within the error interval
around `$L$`.  This can all be done on a graph without any arithmetic or
inequalities discussed.  In the next round, the error player reduces
the allowable error, and the tolerance player adjusts his interval to
get `$f(x)$` within the error.

The number `$L$` is the limit if no matter what error is a small enough
tolerance can be found.

You can discuss the ways a limit can fail to exist also in terms of
the error-tolerance game.

* You can have an ambivalence of limits.  This is a good time to
  introduce the concept of one-sided limits.  They will probably have
  this terminology; show how to adjust the definition.  In the game,
  either one of the one-sided limits could be the limit, but if
  they're different, it's impossible to get arbitrarily close to both
  of them.

A limit will fail to exist if the one-sided limits exist and disagree.

* You can have unboundedness.  If values of `$f(x)$` get arbitrarily
  large near `$a$` they can't get arbitrarily "close" to anything.

* Finally, you can whip out :latex:`$f(x) = \sin(\pi/x)$` near 0.  This is an
  advanced example, to be sure; you can use it to challenge those who
  really know what's going on.  The point of this example is that it
  hits every value between -1 and 1 infinitely many times in any
  neighborhood of 0.  Oy!



Lesson 4: Calculating Limits
============================

:MW Date: January 28
:TR Date: January 29
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 1.4

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Know basic limits like :latex:`$\lim_{x\to a} x = a$` and
  :latex:`$\lim_{x\to a} c = c$`.

* Use the limit laws to compute elementary limits.

* Use algebra to simplify limits.

* Understand and state the Squeeze Theorem.

* Use the Squeeze Theorem to demonstrate a limit.

Notes
-----

The basic limits should be easy.  Then state the limit laws, hand-wave
through justifying them, emphasizing their simplicity.  The only
exception is the quotient rule, which has the caveat that the limit of
the denominator can't be zero.  Of course, that's where all the fun
is!

The big result here is that polynomials are continuous, and rational
functions are continuous wherever there's defined (denominator is not
zero).  You can say *polynomial*, but don't say *continuous*.  That's
for the next time.

The Squeeze theorem is also a pretty fundamental result.  You can use
it to show the existence of certain limits, and we will use it later
on when we prove the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.  Students will
wonder how to come up with the magic bounds; this is a good time to
remind them that :latex:`$|\sin x|\leq 1$` for all `$x$`. [#]_

.. [#] My experience is that calculus students get nervous the exact
       minute the < sign goes on the board.  When you start thinking
       of inequalities, you introduce nonlinear thinking and more than
       one correct answer.  This can be unsettling to novices.  So be
       aware of that.

The Squeeze Theorem is used here to prove that :latex:`$\lim_{x\to
0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$`.  The fundamental inequality here is that
for :latex:`$x<\pi/2$`, :latex:`$\sin x < x < \tan x$`.  Show the
picture (Figure 8 on page 42) to justify that.


Lesson 5: Continuity
====================

:MW Date: February 2
:TR Date: February 3
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 1.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand and apply the definition of *continuity* for a function
  at a point or on an interval.

* Given a piecewise defined function, decide where it is continuous or
  discontinuous.

* State and understand the Intermediate Value Theorem.

* Use the Intermediate Value Theorem to show that a function takes a
  certain value.

* Use the Intermediate Value Theorem to show that an equation has a
  solution.

* Use the Intermediate Value Theorem on conceptual functions.


Notes
-----

Definition
~~~~~~~~~~

Students have an intuitive concept of continuity: you can draw the
graph "without picking up your pencil." See if you can move them
towards a definition involving limits.

We've already determined that rational functions are continuous (on
their dowmains); go over the other elementary functions: other powers,
roots, trigonometric functions, inverse trigonometric function,
exponential functions, and logarithmic functions.  All of these are
continuous on their domains.

So what functions aren't continuous?  You either need the limit to not
exist at a point, or the limit exists but is different from the
function value.  The last is called a *removable discontinuity*;
the more interesting continuity failures are the asymptotes and jump
discontinuities.

You can go over some of the famous functions with discontinuities: the
greatest integer function, the Heaviside function, or the signum
function.

Intermediate Value Theorem
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

Here are some discussion questions to get started:

* Were you ever exactly three feet tall?

* Was your height (in inches) every equal to your weight (in pounds)?

* Is there a pair of points on opposite sides of the world at the same
  temperature at the same time?


The IVT is one of the most important "topological" theorems in
calculus.  We can't prove it (it relies on the fact that closed
intervals are connected), but you can justify it kind of like this: if
the function is continuous on `$[a,b]$`, the the graph connects
`$(a,f(a))$` and `$(b,f(b))$` by a "string." A horizontal line between
`$f(a)$` and `$f(b)$` must be crossed by this string, unless you cut it
which breaks continuity.

Once you've established that, you can prove that :latex:`$\sqrt{2}$`
is a real number by applying the IVT to :latex:`$f(x) = x^2$` on
`$[1,2]$`.  Notice that applying the IVT to :latex:`$f(x) = \sqrt{x}$`
is putting the cart before the horse: we can't say that's continuous
without knowing if it has values at non-square integers.

Now ask again: were you ever exactly three feet tall?

Then give them an equation and show how to construct a function so
that the function takes a specific value at a solution of the
equation.  If the equation is :latex:`$f(x) = g(x)$`, try the function
:latex:$h(x) =f(x) - g(x)$`.  That's obvious to you and me but it
probably wasn't until the first time we saw it.  Now you can tackle
the height vs. weight question.

Now ponder the last question if you have time...


Lesson 6: Limits Involving Infinity
===================================

:MW Date: February 4
:TR Date: February 5
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 1.6

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* "Intuit" limits involving infinity by eyeballing the expression.

* Show limits involving infinity by algebraic manipulation and
  conceptual argument.

Notes
-----

Limits at infinity
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

I think our students should have a facility and familiarity with
algebraic expressions, knowing which are the most important (i.e.,
dominant).  For instance, given a limit like
:latex:`$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2 + 17x -12}{12x^2 + 3x - 17}$$`,
the student should be able to quickly decide that the limit is 1/4 by
looking at the coefficients of the highest order terms.  They should
also be able to algebraically manipulate this expression by factoring
out :latex:`$x^2$` from the numerator and denominator, canceling, and
using the fact that :latex:`$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{1}{x} = 0$`.  We
want to them to both know the answer and be able to demonstrate it.

Here's a good time to talk about my favorite horizontal asymptote:
:latex:`$\lim_{x\to\infty}\arctan x = \frac{\pi}{2}$`.

Infinite Limits
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

It's pretty clear that :latex:`$\lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{1}{x}$` does not
exist.  As `$x$` gets smaller, :latex:`$\frac{1}{x}$` gets larger.  In
fact, no matter how large we want :latex:`$\frac{1}{x}$` to be, we can
make `$x$` small enough to guarantee :latex:`$\frac{1}{x}$` is larger.
You can tell them that this fundamental concept of real numbers is due
to Archimedes.

This leads to an extension of the definition of limit to include the
possibility of an infinite limit.  A vertical asymptote is the
graphical manifestation of an infinite limit.

Now go over the functions which have infinite limits at limit points
of their domain: rational functions, sec, tan, csc, cot, and so on.
Also make sure you help them discover that :latex:`$\lim_{x\to 0^+}
\ln x = -\infty$`.  They never remember that one.

Finally, you can go over the limit laws again but warn them that some
of them apply when the limits are infinite, and some do not.  For
instance, what should :latex:`$\lim_{x\to 0^+} \left(\frac{1}{x} -
\frac{1}{x^2}\right)$` be?  Infinite limits are a special case of a
limit failing to exist, rather than an extension of the concept of a
limit succeeding in existing.  Otherwise, we'd have to go back and
rewrite the limit laws to exclude all the bad arithmetic with
`$\infty$`.

Lesson 8: The Derivative as a Function
======================================

:MW Date: February 9–11
:TR Date: February 10–12
:Duration: two-thirds of one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 2.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Given a function `$f$`, use the definition of the derivative to find the
  derivative function *f'*.

* Given a function, find its *second derivative.*

* Given the graph of a function, sketch the graph of its derivative.

Notes
-----

The previous section is about the derivative at a point.  This section
is about the *derived function* of `$f$`, the function `$f'$`.

An important theorem is `$f$` is continuous at `$a$` when it's
differentiable at `$a$`. The proof is probably accessible by them, but
may not be so well appreciated, so you can skip it if you feel it.
Merely impressing upon them that differentiability is "super"
continuity is enough.

Second and higher derivatives are in this section, too.  You can also
talk about Newtonian and Leibnizian notation for derivatives.

Lesson 9: Basic Differentiation Rules
=====================================

:MW Date: February 11
:TR Date: February 12
:Duration: two-thirds of one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 2.3


Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand and use these differentiation rules:

  - the derivative of a constant function (zero);

  - the Constant Multiple Rule;

  - the Sum Rule;

  - the Difference Rule;

  - the derivatives of sine and cosine.


Notes
-----

Here we start on "the calculus of derivatives," from which the entire
subject gets its name (and perhaps leading to the misconception that
calculus is all about taking derivatives!)

The first is the power rule.  Stewart shows it for positive integers
then declares it for all other real powers.  We'll allow it but I
still want to emphasize the different proofs for the different powers
(using the quotient rule for negative integers, using implicit
differentiation for rational numbers, and finally logarithmic
differentiation for irrational numbers.

Do the sum and constant multiple rules, pointing out that this is
really just the limit laws applied the the difference quotients.

The hard work for the derivative of sine (i.e., the fact that
:latex:`$\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x}=1$` is done in Section 1.3.

Lesson 11: The Chain Rule
=========================

:MW Date: February 23
:TR Date: February 19
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 2.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Given a compound expression, write it as a composotion of functions.

* Understand and apply the *Chain Rule* for the derivative of a
  composition of functions.

* Understand and use Newtonian and Leibnizian notations for the Chain Rule.

Notes
-----

My metaphor here isn't as good as that for the product rule, but it
goes like this.  If you're riding a multi-speed bicycle, the speed of
the rear wheel depends on how fast you're pedaling as well as the gear
you're in.  Now if you can imagine a front sprocket which is not round
you can see how the angular velocity of the rear wheel depends on the
rate of change of the radius of the front sprocket.  No matter what
speed you're in, pedaling twice as fast will double your speed, so
that explains the product of the two.

Part of the task is going to be recognizing a function as a
composition of simpler functions.  I use machines as a metaphor, where
the output of one feeds into the input of another.  Once you have this
decomposition, the derivative of the composition is the product of the
derivatives, and it's also clear here where these derivatives are
evaluated: at the point that's being input to the original function.

Lesson 13: Linear Approximations and Differentials
==================================================

:MW Date: March 2
:TR Date: February 26
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 2.8

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Use tangent lines to make linear approximations to a function.

  * Given a function and a point in the domain, compute the
    linearization of the function at that point.

  * Use linearization to approximate values of functions

* Given a function, compute the differential of that function

* Use the differential notation to estimate error in linear
  approximations.

Notes
-----

Linear approximation is basically equivalent to differentiation.  The
line that intersects a graph at a point and best approximates the
function near that point is exactly the tangent line.  But sometimes
students miss that connection and think of linear approximation as a
completely different thing.

Linear approximation is useful in estimating values of functions at
inconvenient points near convenient points.  For instance,
:latex:`$\sqrt{5}$` is hard but :latex:`$\sqrt{4}$` is easy.  So
linearly approximate at 4.

Differentials are used here as an alternate notation for linear
approximations.  If `$y=f(x)$` is evaluated at `$x$` and `$x+dx$`,
then :latex:`$\Delta y = f(x+dx) - f(x)$` is approximately equal to
:latex:`$dy = f'(x) dx$` So if you need to bound `$\Delta y$` you can find a
suitable `$dx$`.

Lesson 19: The Mean Value Theorem
=================================

:MW Date: March 25
:TR Date: March 26
:Duration: one-half of one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 4.2

* Understand and be able to explain the statement of *Rolle's Theorem*.

* Understand and be able to explain the statement of the *Mean Value
  Theorem*.

Notes
-----

Rolle's Theorem is simply an application of Fermat's Theorem.  The
Mean Value Theorem comes from Rolle's Theorem applied to a jury-rigged
function.

*The Mean Value Theorem is the most important theorem in calculus.* It
is the theorem which ties derivative information to information about
the original function.  From the MVT we prove that a function with
zero derivative (on an interval) must be a constant, or that two
functions with the same derivative (again, on an interval), differ by
a constant.  The MVT is used in the proof of the FTC.

Lesson 22: Optimization Problems
================================

:MW Date: April 6–8
:TR Date: April 7–9
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 4.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Given a problem requiring optimization, identify the objective
  functions, variables, and constraints.

* Solve optimization problems with calculus.

Notes
-----

One of the toughest parts of optimization problems is the modeling of a word
problem into a math problem.  So take it slow and emphasize the problem-solving
process.  As with graphing, we'll spend a week on this topic.

The process uses a lot of relations that students know but don't
remember or think to use, in order to reduce the number of variables
to one: the Pythagorean theorem and similar triangles among them.  Be
patient.

Like the related rates sections, I'm inclined to start with simple
problems, done completely by you, then gradually nudging them towards
doing their own problems in groups.

In the second half of class, I'm probably going to give them a worksheet with
two extended optimization problems, one of them being the "Statue of
Liberty problem": how far away from the statue should you stand to
maximize the size of the statue in your vision?



Lesson 23: Areas and Distances
==============================

:MW Date: April 13
:TR Date: April 14
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 5.1

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Compute the area of a region by approximating it with rectangles and
  letting the size of the rectangles tend to zero.

* Compute the total distance traveled by a particle by approximating
  it as distance = (rate)(time) and letting the time intervals over
  which one approximates tend to zero.

Notes
-----

The similarity between the two processes is a big idea.  We have
simple "product" formulas like area = (length)(width) and distance =
(rate)(time), or mass = (density)(length), and so on.  When the things
we are trying to multiply are no longer constants but functions, we
divide and conquer: divide into small intervals along which one of the
functions can be reasonably approximated by a constant.  Then let the
intervals tend to zero in width.

Lesson 24: The Definite Integral
================================

:MW Date: April 15
:TR Date: April 16
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 5.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Understand the definition of the *definite integral* of a function
  function over a closed interval.

* Use the Midpoint Rule to estimate definite integrals.

* Understand and use the linearity and comparison properties of the
  definite integral.

Notes
-----

In some sense calculus is all about limits of indeterminate form.
Derivatives are limits of the form 0/0, while integrals are limits of
the form 0 |times| |infinity| .

Property 8 for definite integrals is a key step in the proof of the
First Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.



Lesson 25: Evaluating Definite Integrals
========================================

:MW Date: April 20
:TR Date: April 21
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 5.3

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Use the Evaluation Theorem to evaluate definite integrals.

* Write antiderivatives as *indefinite integrals*.

* Interpret definite integrals as "net change" of a function over an
  interval.

Notes
-----

Two things I don't like about this section: the second Fundamental
Theorem of Calculus comes first, and Stewart gives it a new name.

But that doesn't mean it's not important.  You might motivate this by
using distance and velocity instead of area: We know the derivative of
distance is velocity, so the antiderivative of velocity is distance,
but the integral of velocity is distance as well.  So integration and
antidifferentiation are somehow related.

This is the climax of Calc I in some sense, so it's reasonable to
prove this.

There are two proofs of FTCII.  The weaker version comes
from the first Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, namely that
:latex:`$\frac{d}{dx} \int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)$`.  If `$F$` is another
antiderivative of `$f$`, then :latex:`$F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt + C$`. 
Since :latex:`$\int_a^af(t)\,dt = 0$`, we have `$C=F(a)$` and we're done.

This proof requires `$f$` to be continuous, which isn't so bad because
most of the functions we discuss will be continuous.  The more general
proof using very well chosen Riemann sums is in the book.  If you can
show both, do so.

The big point is that antidifferentiation has another use, now for
computing areas.  Which means we had better get more serious about
collecting antiderivatives.  So start writing up a simple integration
table.

Then there are all the other interpretations of "Net Change:" If `$f$`
is velocity it's distance.  If `$f$` is density it's mass.  If `$f$` is
marginal cost it's total cost.  And so on.


Lesson 26: The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
==============================================

:MW Date: April 23
:TR Date: April 24
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 5.4


Learning objectives
-------------------

* State and explain the Fundemental Theorems of Calculus

* Use the first fundamental theorem of calculus to find derivatives of
  functions defined as integrals.

* Compute the average value of an integrable function over a closed interval.

Notes
-----

This is the other fundamental theorem of calculus, of course.  To
motivate it, consider a given function's *area function* :latex:`$F(x)
= \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$`.  What does the graph of `$F(x)$` look like for
various `$f$`?  Students should be able to figure out that when `$f$` is
positive, `$F$` is increasing, and when `$f$` is negative, `$F$` is
decreasing, and so on.  So `$F$` starts to "look like" an antiderivative
for `$F$`.

Now you can state and prove the theorem.  Emphasize that we have do
back to the definition of derivative for `$F$`, that is, take the limit
of difference quotients.  We have 

.. latex::

  \frac{F(a+h)-F(a)}{h} = \frac{1}{h} \int_a^{x+h} f(t)\,dt

By Property 8 in Section 5.2, :latex:`$\int_a^{a+h} f(t)\,dt$` is
bounded above by the maximum of `$f$` on `$[a,a+h]$` times `$h$`, and
bounded below by the minimum of `$f$` on `$[a,a+h]$` times `$h$`.  So
the difference quotient itself if bounded between the max and min of
`$f$` on`$[a,a+h]$`.  So `$F'(a)$`, if it exists, is between the lim
sup and lim inf of `$f$` as `$x$` approaches `$a$`. But since `$f$` is
continuous at `$a$`, the limit does exist and is `$f(a)$`.  (Squeeze
Theorem!)

Now we get to one of the most confusing simple tasks in calculus:
differentiating a function expressed as an integral.  It shouldn't be,
but it is.  You can get them to repeat that
:latex:`$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)$`, but they will get
completely confused with :latex:`$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{x^2} f(t)\,dt =
f(x^2)(2x)$`.  Perhaps try to hide the integral and call the area
function `$F$`; then it's clear that :latex:`$\frac{d}{dx}F(x^2) =
F'(x^2)(2x)$`.  Students also will try to work :latex:`$f'$` in here,
but point out that the FTC never involves the derivative of the
integrand.

This seems like a pointless task, but there are functions which are
defined in terms of an integral, such as the error function
:latex:`$\int_{0}^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$`, the sine integral function 
:latex:`$\int_0^x\frac{\sin t}{t}\,dt$`, and the Fresnel function.


Lesson 27: The Substitution Rule
================================

:MW Date: April 27
:TR Date: April 28
:Duration: one class period
:See Also: Stewart_, Section 5.5

Prequisites
-----------

* The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

* Even and odd functions


Learning Objectives
-------------------

* Given an integral and a substitution, transform the integral into an
  equivalent one using a substitution

* Evaluate indefinite integrals using the method of substitution.

* Evaluate definite integrals using the method of substitution.

* Use symmetry to simplify integrals of even and odd functions.

Notes
-----

Students are going to get caught in many tiny traps the first time
around.

You should do both the substitution rule for indefinite and definite
integrals.  Many will not like the definite integral version; they are
content to do the indefinite integral version then plug in.  But
emphasize that the definite integral version allows them to totally
simplify the integral then move on without going back to the original
one.

The way I explain how the the limits change in 

.. latex:: 

   \int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x) = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du 

is that the integral on the left happens in ":latex:`$x$`\ -land."  The
variable is `$x$` and the limits are an interval in `$x$`.  The
integral on the right is in "`$u$`\ -land," so the limits should be
values of `$u$` as well.  How do you get from `$x$` to `$u$`?  Well,
the substitution was `$u=g(x)$`.  So the `$u$`-values corresponding to
`$x=a$` and `$x=b$` are `$u=g(a)$` and `$u=g(b)$`.

Students will trip up themselves when making the substitution.  They
will forget to take care of the `$dx$` and still have it in the
substituted integral.

Finally, this is a nonlinear problem so students aren't going to know
right away what makes a good `$u$` in substitution.  Remind them that
whatever you make `$u$`, you need to "see" `$u'$` in the integrand, in the
numerator of the integrand if it's a fraction, in order for the
substitution to even work.  So see if you can get them to think about
function/derivative pairs, the obvious ones being :latex:`$x^n$` with
:latex:`$x^{n-1}$` (modulo constants), or sin with cos, and the non-obvious
ones being :latex:`$e^x$` with  :latex:`$e^x$`, :latex:`$\sqrt{x}$` with
:latex:`$\frac{1}{\sqrt x}$`, and :latex:`$\ln x$` with :latex:`$\frac{1}{x}$`.

Substitution applied to symmetric (even or odd) functions gives nice
simplification for definite integrals over intervals which are
symmetric about zero.  The integral of an odd function over such an
interval is zero; while the integral of an even function over such an
interval is twice the integral over the second half.  A picture is
most convincing here, the substitution `$u=-x$` makes it rigorous.
