Lesson 1: Three-dimensional Coordinate Systems

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 10.1

Learning Objectives

Lesson 2: Vectors

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 10.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to apply the axioms or vector addition and scalar multiplication.

* To understand the definitions of *unit vectors*, *standard basis

Lesson 3: The Dot Product
=========================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 10.3

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to use the definitions of the *dot product* to measure the length of a vector and the angle :latex:`$\theta$` between two vectors.  Given two vectors :latex:`${\mathbf a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle $` and :latex:`${\mathbf b} = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle$`,

.. latex::

   {\mathbf a} \cdot {\mathbf b} = |{\mathbf a}| |{\mathbf b}| \cos \theta  
   = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3.

* To understand and be able to apply properties of the dot product.

* To understand that two vectors are *orthogonal* if their dot product is zero.

* To understand and be able to determine the *projection* of a vector :latex:`${\mathbf a}$` onto a vector :latex:`${\mathbf b}$`,

.. latex::

   \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf a }{\mathbf b} = \frac{ {\mathbf a} \cdot {\mathbf
   b}}{|{\mathbf a}|^2} {\mathbf a}

* To be able to use vectors and the dot product in applications.



Lesson 4: The Cross Product
===========================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 10.4

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to use the definitions of the *cross
  product* to find a vector that is orthogoal two vectors.  Given two
  vectors :latex:`$\mathbf{a} = \langle a_1, a_2, a_3 \rangle $` and
  :latex:`$\mathbf{b} = \langle b_1, b_2, b_3 \rangle$`,

.. latex::

   \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b} =( |\mathbf{a}| |\mathbf{b}| \sin \theta )   
   \mathbf{n} = 
   \langle a_2 b_3 - a_3 b_2, a_3 b_1 - a_1 b_3,  a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1 \rangle,


where :latex:`$\mathbf{n}$` is a unit vector perpendicular to both :latex:`$\mathbf{a}$` and :latex:`$\mathbf{b}$`

* To understand and be able to apply properties of the dot product, especially :latex:`$\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}  = - \mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{a}$`.

* To understand that two vectors are parallel if and only if their cross product is zero.

* To be able to use vectors and the cross product in applications.


Lesson 5: Equations of Lines and Planes
=======================================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 10.5


Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to represent a line :latex:`$\ell$` in
  :latex:`${\mathbb R}^3$` using
 
  - the *vector equation*
    
    .. latex::

       {\mathbf r} = {\mathbf r}_0 + t {\mathbf v}

  - the *parametric equations*

    .. latex::

       x = x_0 + at \qquad y = y_0 + bt \qquad z = z_0 + ct

  - the *symmetric equations* 

    .. latex::

       \frac{x - x_0}{a} = \frac{y - y_0}{b} = \frac{z - z_0}{c} 

* To be able to represent a line segment in :latex:`${\mathbb R}^3$`
  from :latex:`${\mathbf r}_0$` to :latex:`${\mathbf r}_1$`,

  .. latex::
  
   {\mathbf r}(t) = (1 - t) {\mathbf r}_0 + t {\mathbf r}_1,

  where :latex:`$0 \leq t \leq 1$`.


* To understand and be able to represent a plane in :latex:`${\mathbb
  R}^3$` given a normal vector :latex:`${\mathbf n} = \langle a, b, c
  \rangle$` and a point :latex:`$P_0 = (x_0, y_0, z_0)$` in the
  plane,

  .. latex::

     {\mathbf n} \cdot \langle x - x_0, y - y_0, z - z_0 \rangle = 0

  or 

  .. latex::

     a(x - x_0) + b( y - y_0) + c ( z - z_0) = 0.

* To understand and be able to calculate the distance :latex:`$D$`
  from a point :latex:`$P_1 = (x_1, y_1, z_1)$` to the plane
  :latex:`$ax + by + cz + d = 0$`,

  .. latex::

     D = \frac{|a x_1 + b y_1 + cz_1 + d|}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}.

Lesson 8: Arc length
====================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 10.8

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to apply the arc length of a curve,
  `${\mathbf r}(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t) \rangle$`, over an
  interval `$a \leq t \leq b$`,

  .. latex::

     L = \int_a^b |{\mathbf r}'(t) | \, dt = \int_a^b \sqrt{ [f(t)]^2 + [g(t)]^2 + [h(t)]^2} \, dt.

* To understand and be able to apply the arc length function of a
  curve, `${\mathbf r}(t) = \langle f(t), g(t), h(t) \rangle$`, over
  an interval `$a \leq t \leq b$`,

  .. latex::

     s(t) = \int_a^t |{\mathbf r}'(u) | \, du = \int_a^t \sqrt{ [f(u)]^2 + [g(u)]^2 + [h(u)]^2} \, du.

Lesson 16: Maximum and Minimum Values
=====================================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 11.7

Learning Objectives
-------------------

*  Use the second derivative test to classify critical points of functions

Notes
-----

When I teach this section, I remind students how we find maximum and
minimum values of a function of one variable.  We find the critical
points where the derivative is zero, and the second derivative tells
us concavity.  A positive second derivative means a concave-up graph
(looks like an upward-opening parabola), which means a local minimum.
A negative second derivative means a concave-down graph (looks like a
downward-opening parabola), which means a local maximum.

In two variables, however, there's more than one second derivative,
and there's more than one quadratic function.  What combination of the
second derivatives distinguishes the graphs of `$z=x^2 + y^2$`,
`$z=-x^2-y^2$`, and `$z=x^2-y^2$`, but classifies `$z=x^2-y^2$` and
`$z=xy$` similarly?  Unless you complete the square and show them
explicitly, they are probably not going to figure out that the
determinant comes into play (and maybe not even then), but I think
it's important that they see where the magic formulas come from.

Lesson 17: Lagrange Multipliers
===============================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 11.8

Learning Objectives
-------------------

*  Use Lagrangian Multipliers to solve constrained optimization problems

*  Understand why Lagrangian Multipliers "work."

*  Solve nonlinear systems of equations.

Notes
-----

Economics is all about constrained optimization, so try to do an
example from that field as well.

Students are going to have major difficulties solving nonlinear
systems of equations.  Their first strategy is to eliminate a variable
by isolating it in one of the equations and substituting it back into
the others.  But this can get kind of messy.  Show them how to take
combinations of equations that simplify, for instance dividing
equations when they both have a `$\lambda$` as a factor on one side.

Lesson 19: Double Integrals over General Regions
================================================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 12.2

* To understand and be able to compute the double integral of a
  function `$f$` over a general region `$D$`,

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_{d} f(x, y) \, dA.


* To understand and be able to compute the double integral of a
  function `$f$` over a *type I* region `$R$`,

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_{D} f(x, y) \, dA = \int_a^b \int
     _{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)} f(x, y) \, dy \, dx,

  where

  .. latex::

     D = \{ (x, y) | a \leq x \leq b, g_1(x) \leq y \leq g_2(x) \}.

* To understand and be able to compute the double integral of a
  function `$f$` over a *type II* region `$R$`,

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_{D} f(x, y) \, dA = \int_c^d \int
     _{h_1(y)}^{h_2(y)} f(x, y) \, dx \, dy,

  where

  .. latex::

     D = \{ (x, y) | c \leq y \leq d, h_1(y) \leq x \leq h_2(y) \}.


* To understand and be able to apply the properties of double integrals.

  - :latex:`$\displaystyle\iint \limits_{D} [f(x, y) + g(x, y)] \, dA = \iint
    \limits_{D} f(x, y) \, dA + \iint \limits_{D} g(x, y) \, dA$`

  - :latex:`$\displaystyle\iint \limits_{D} c f(x, y) \, dA = c \iint\limits_{D} f(x, y) \, dA$`

  - If `$f( x, y) \geq g(x, y)$` for all `$(x, y) \in D$`, then
    `$\displaystyle \iint \limits_{D} f(x, y) \, dA \geq \iint
    \limits_{D} g(x, y) \, dA$`.

  - If `$D = D_1 \cup D_2$`, where `$D_1$` and `$D_2$` do not overlap
    except possibly on their boundaries, then `$\displaystyle \iint
    \limits_{D} [f(x, y) ] \, dA = \iint \limits_{D_1} f(x, y) \, dA +
    \iint \limits_{D_2} f(x, y) \, dA$`

  - `$\displaystyle \iint \limits_{D} dA$` is the area of `$D$`,
    `$A(D)$`.

  - If `$m \leq f( x, y) \geq M$` for all `$(x, y) \in D$`, then
    `$\displaystyle m \cdot A(D) \leq \iint \limits_{D} f(x, y) \, dA
    \leq M \cdot A(D)$`.

Lesson 21: Triple Intgrals

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 12.5

Learning Objectives

  integral of a function `$f$` over a box `$B$`,

  .. latex::

     \iiint \limits_{B} f(x, y, z) \, dV = \lim_{l, m, n \to \infty}

  .. latex::

     \iiint \limits_{B} f(x, y, z) \, dV

* To understand and to be able to compute the value of

  .. latex::

     \iiint \limits_{E} f(x, y, z) \, dV = \int_a^b

  .. latex::

     E = \{ a \leq x \leq b, g_1(x) \leq y \leq g_2(x), u_1(x, y) \leq
     z \leq u_2(x, y)\}.

* To understand and to be able to compute the value of

  .. latex::

     \iiint \limits_{E} f(x, y, z) \, dV = \int_c^d

     dz \, dx \, dy

  when 

  .. latex::

     E = \{ c \leq y \leq d, h_1(y) \leq x \leq h_2(y), u_1(x, y) \leq

  .. latex::

Lesson 25: Line Integrals
=========================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.2

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to apply the definition of a line integral
  of a function :latex:`$f$` along a curve :latex:`$C$`,

  .. latex::

     \int_C f(x, y) \, ds = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{i = 1}^n f(x_i^*,
     y_i^*) \Delta s_i.


* To be able to compute a line integral of a function :latex:`$f$`
  along a curve :latex:`$C$` using the formula

  .. latex::

     \int_C f(x, y) \, ds = \int_a^b f(x(t), y(t)) \sqrt{\left(
     \frac{dx}{dt} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{dy}{dt} \right)^2} \, dt,

  where :latex:`$C$` is parameterized by :latex:`$(x(t), y(t))$` with
  :latex:`$a \leq t \leq b$`.  To be able to extend this computation
  to curves in :latex:`${\mathbb R}^3$`.

* To be able to compute the line integral over a vector field
  :latex:`${\mathbf F}$` along a curve :latex:`$C$` parameterized
  :latex:`$\mathbf{r}(t)$`,

  .. latex::

     \int_C {\mathbf F} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b {\mathbf F}(\mathbf{r}(t))
     \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) \, dt = \int_C P \, dx + Q \, dy + R \, dz,

  where :latex:`${\mathbf F} = P \mathbf{i} + Q \mathbf{j} + R \mathbf{k}$`.

Lesson 26: The Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals
====================================================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.3

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* If :latex:`$C$` is a smooth curve given by :latex:`$\mathbf{r}(t)$` for :latex:`$a \leq t \leq b$`,

  .. latex::

     \int_C \nabla f \cdot d\mathbf{r} = f(\mathbf{r}(b)) - f(\mathbf{r}(a)).

  In particular, the line integral of a {\em conservative vector
  field\/} depends only on the initial and terminal points of :latex:`$C$`.

* The line integral :latex:`$\int_C {\mathbf F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$` is path
  independent in :latex:`$D$` if and only if :latex:`$\int_C {\mathbf F} \cdot
  d\mathbf{r} = 0$` for every closed path in :latex:`$C$`.

* If :latex:`${\mathbf F}$` is a continuous vector field on an open connected
  region :latex:`$D$` and :latex:`$\int_C {\mathbf F} \cdot d\mathbf{r}$` is path
  independent in :latex:`$D$`, :latex:`${\mathbf F}$` is a conservative vector field.

* If :latex:`${\mathbf F}(x, y) = P(x, y) \, \mathbf{i} + Q(x, y) \,
  \mathbf{j}$` is conservative,

  .. latex::

     \frac{\partial P}{\partial y} = \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}.
 
* If :latex:`${\mathbf F}(x, y) = P(x, y) \, \mathbf{i} + Q(x, y) \,
  \mathbf{j}$` is a vector field on an open *simply connected* region
  :latex:`$D$` and :latex:`$\partial P / \partial y = \partial Q /
  \partial x$`, then :latex:`${\mathbf F}$` is a conservative vector
  field.

Lesson 27: Green's Theorem
==========================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.4

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to apply *Green's Theorem*: Let `$C$` be a
  positively oriented, piecewise-smooth, simple closed curve in
  `${\mathbb R}^2$` and `$D$` be the region bounded by `$C$`.  If `$P$` and
  `$Q$` have continuous partial derivatives on an open region that
  contains `$D$`, then

  .. latex::

     \int_C P \, dx + Q \, dy = \iint \limits_D \left( \frac{\partial
     Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) \, dA.

* To understand and be able to apply Green's Theorem for a region
  `$R$` with "holes": Let `$D$` be the region in `${\mathbb R}^2$`
  bounded by `$C_1$`, a positively oriented, piecewise-smooth, simple
  closed outer curve, and `$C_2$`, a positively oriented,
  piecewise-smooth, simple closed inner curve.  Then

  .. latex::

     \int_{C_1} P \, dx + Q \, dy - \int_{C_2} P \, dx + Q \, dy =
     \iint \limits_D \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} -
     \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right) \, dA.

Lesson 28: Curl and Divergence
==============================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.5

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be apply the definition of the *curl* of a vector field 

  .. latex::

     {\mathbf F} = P \, \mathbf{i} + Q \, \mathbf{j} + R \, \mathbf{k}

  in `${\mathbb R}^3$`,

  .. latex::

     \mathrm{curl} {\mathbf F} = \left( \frac{\partial R}{\partial y} -
     \frac{\partial Q}{\partial z} \right) \mathbf{i} + \left( \frac{\partial
     P}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial R}{\partial x} \right) \mathbf{j} +
     \left( \frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial
     y} \right) \mathbf{k}.

  This is most easily remembered as

  .. latex::

     \mathrm{curl} {\mathbf F} = \nabla \times {\mathbf F}.

  One of the main properties of the curl is

  .. latex::

     \mathrm{curl}( \nabla f ) = {\mathbf 0}.


* To understand and be apply the definition of the *divergence* of a vector field 

  .. latex::

      {\mathbf F} = P \, \mathbf{i} + Q \, \mathbf{j} + R \, \mathbf{k}

  in `${\mathbb R}^3$`,

  .. latex::

     \operatorname{div} {\mathbf F} = \frac{\partial P}{\partial x} +
     \frac{\partial Q}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial R}{\partial y}.
 
  This is most easily remembered as

  .. latex::

     \operatorname{div} {\mathbf F} = \nabla \cdot {\mathbf F}.

One of the main properties of the curl is

.. latex::

   \operatorname{div} \mathrm{curl} {\mathbf F} = 0.


* To understand when a vector field is *conservative*, *irrotational*,
  or *incompressible*.

Lesson 29: Parametric Surfaces and their Areas
==============================================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.6

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to apply the concept of a *parametric
  surface*.  A parametric surface may be defined by a vector equation,

  .. latex::

     \mathbf{r}(t) = x(u, v) \, \mathbf{i} + y(u, v) \, \mathbf{j} + z(u, v) \, \mathbf{k}

  or by a set of parametric equations

  .. latex::

     x = x(u, v) \qquad y = y(u, v) \qquad z = z(u, v),

  where `$u$` and `$v$` are variables with a domain `$D$` contained in `${\mathbb R}^2$`.

* To understand and be able to represent surfaces of revolutions parametrically.

* To understand and be able to compute the surface area of a parameterized surface

  .. latex::

     \mathbf{r}(u, v) = x(u, v) \mathbf{i} + y(u, v) \mathbf{j} + z(u, v) \mathbf{k}

  where :latex:`$(u, v)$` are in a domain :latex:`$D$`.

  .. latex::

     A(S) = \iint \limits_{D} |\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v| \, dA,

  where

  .. latex::

     \mathbf{r}_u = \frac{\partial x}{\partial u} \mathbf{i} +
     \frac{\partial y}{\partial u} \mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial
     z}{\partial u} \mathbf{k} \qquad \mathbf{r}_v = \frac{\partial
     x}{\partial v} \mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial y}{\partial v}
     \mathbf{j} + \frac{\partial z}{\partial v} \mathbf{k}

* To understand and be able to compute the surface area of the graph
  of a function :latex:`$z = f(x, y)$`,

  .. latex::

     A(S) = \iint \limits_{D} \sqrt{1 + \left( \frac{\partial
     z}{\partial x} \right)^2 + \left( \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}
     \right)^2} \, dA.

* To understand and be able to compute the surface area of a surface
  of revolution :latex:`$S$` obtained by rotating a curve :latex:`$y =
  f(x)$`, :latex:`$a \leq x \leq b$`, about the :latex:`$x$`-axis,
  where :latex:`$f(x) \geq 0$` and :latex:`$f'$` is continuous,

  .. latex::

     A(S) = 2 \pi \int_a^b f(x) \sqrt{1 + [f'(x)]^2} \, dx.

Lesson 30: Surface Integrals
============================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.7

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand the definition of and be able to compute surface integrals,

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_S f(x, y, z) \, dS = \iint \limits_D f(\mathbf{r}(u, v) )
     | \mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v | \, dA,

  where `$f$` is a function defined on the parametric surface `$\mathbf{r}(u,
  v)$`.  If `$S$` is defined by `$z = g(x, y)$`, then

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_S f(x, y, z) \, dS = \iint \limits_D f(x, y,
     g(x,y)) \sqrt{ \left( \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \right)^2 +
     \left( \frac{\partial z}{\partial y} \right)^2+ 1}\, dA.


* To understand and be able to apply the concept of an oriented surface.

* To understand and be able to apply the definition of flux integral
  of a vector field across a surface `$S$`.  Let `${\mathbf F}$` be a
  continuous vector field on an oriented surface `$S$` with unit
  normal vector `${\mathbf n}$`.  The surface integral of `${\mathbf
  F}$` over `$S$` is

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_S {\mathbf F} \cdot d{\mathbf S} = \iint \limits_S
     {\mathbf F} \cdot {\mathbf n} \, dS.

  This integral is given by

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_S {\mathbf F} \cdot d{\mathbf S} = \iint \limits_D
     {\mathbf F} \cdot (\mathbf{r}_u \times \mathbf{r}_v) \, dA

  for a parametrically defined surface.  If `$S$` is defined by `$z =
  g(x, y)$` and `${\mathbf F} = P \, \mathbf{i} + Q \, \mathbf{j} + R \, \mathbf{j}$`, then

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_S {\mathbf F} \cdot d{\mathbf S} = \iint \limits_D
     \left( -P \frac{\partial g}{\partial x} - Q \frac{\partial
     g}{\partial y} + R\right)\, dA.

Lesson 31: Stokes's Theorem
===========================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.8

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to apply Stokes's Theorem.  Let `$S$` be an
  oriented piecewise-smooth surface that is bounded by a simple,
  closed piecewise-smooth boundary curve with positive orientation.
  Let `${\mathbf F}$` be a vector field whose components have continuous
  partial derivatives on an open region in `${\mathbb R}^3$` that
  contains `$S$`.  Then

  .. latex::

     \int_C {\mathbf F} \cdot d \mathbf{r} = \iint \limits_S \mathrm{curl} {\mathbf
     F} \cdot d{\mathbf S}.

Lesson 32: (Gauss's) Divergence Theorem
=======================================

:See Also: Stewart_, Section 13.9

Learning Objectives
-------------------

* To understand and be able to apply the Divergence Theorem.  Let `$E$`
  be a simple solid region and let `$S$` be the boundary surface of `$E$`,
  given with positive (outward) orientation. Let `$\mathbf F$` be a vector
  field whose components have continuous partial derivatives on an
  open region in `${\mathbb R}^3$` that contains `$E$`.  Then

  .. latex::

     \iint \limits_S \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \iiint \limits_E
     \operatorname{div} \mathbf{F} \, dV.

Notes
-----

At some point I like to point out that there's really only one
Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (which we in the business know as
Stokes's Theorem).  The one-variable FTC, the Fundamental Theorem of
Line Integrals, Green, Gauss, and Stokes, are all identities which
relate the integral of "some kind of derivative" (exact differential)
over a set (manifold) to the integral of whatever it's the derivative
of (the coboundary) over the boundary of that set (in the inherited
orientation).  Once you understand that, there's really only one way
to line up all these identities.
